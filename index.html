About

Hi, I'm Ivey.

I'm from Austin, and I just moved home after six years in San Francisco, where I was essentially a chief of staff for a managing partner at a large law firm. I loved being the conduit between the technical experts and the rest of the business. I didn't have to be an attorney to know what they needed to work efficiently and produce great work for their clients, but I did need to know how to communicate to them, and from the technical experts to the rest of the organization. There are a lot of parallels between the legal industry and tech: just replace attorneys with engineers or a product team.

I worked for the same boss through four different roles at two firms. Along the way I opened a new office and renovated our San Francisco office in the same nine months (while renovating my own house, which is how I learned to read a change order), put together the pitch deck for our practice group's move to a larger firm, and then ran the transition.

I love the logistics and operations of professional services, and I'm curious what those skills can do outside of them, in tech and supply chain. I tend to be risk averse, so business school is my exercise in pushing out of that comfort zone, because you never know what you're good at until you've had the opportunity to try. I'm trying to find it exhilarating instead of terrifying.

What I'm looking for: a culture of longevity, but not stagnation.

xo, Ivey

McCombs+ consulting project

Fall 2026 · Five-person consulting team · Client confidential under NDA

Our team is advising a lean real estate team at a fast-growing national company on how to scale its fee invoicing, collections, and revenue forecasting without adding headcount. What I'm taking away so far:

  1. Map it before you fix it. We're starting with a judgment-free, as-is map of how money actually moves today, treating the client kickoff as a listening session, before designing the to-be process.
  2. Every milestone should trigger an invoice. Defining clear, event-based invoice triggers, required documents, and an escalation path for late payers turns a reactive process into a predictable one.
  3. Forecast the timing, not just the total. When deals take years to close and pay out in installments, a useful forecast models when cash arrives and how uncertain that is.
  4. Right-size the tools. A one-to-two person team needs a single source of truth and light integrations, not an enterprise platform it can't maintain.
  5. Scope like a consultant. A finite scope inside an hours budget, steady weekly updates instead of a big reveal, and under-promise, over-deliver.
